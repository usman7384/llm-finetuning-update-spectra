\section{Case study: is post-training superficial?}
\label{sec:timber}

\citet{wu2025timber} compare the weights of released base and instruct models
(Llama~3 and Qwen3) through the entropy effective rank of each weight matrix.
They find it nearly identical before and after post-training, at about 85\% of
full rank (interquartile range 0.75--0.95), and read this as weight-level
evidence that post-training is superficial. They also propose an edit of the
instruct model: keep the top $K = \lceil \entropyrank(\dW)\cdot r \rceil$
singular directions of the update and scale the rest by $\lambda < 1$, which
improves pass@$k$ on their benchmarks. We check both with the baselines of
\S\ref{sec:background}.

\subsection{The measurement reproduces}

On Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-8B, two of the pairs in their study, the effective
rank of the base weights is 0.84 and 0.86 of full rank (interquartile ranges
0.76--0.91 and 0.76--0.94), matching their summary. Between base and instruct
it changes by only 0.13\% and 0.06\%.

\subsection{But any small update does the same}

\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{figures/erank_perturbation.pdf}
\end{center}
\caption{Change in the effective rank of the full weight matrix $W$ caused by
the real update (blue) and by a random update of the same size (grey), averaged
over matrices, log scale. Every change, real or random, is below 0.5\%.}
\label{fig:erank}
\end{figure}

For every matrix we compare the change caused by the real update with the
change caused by a random update of the same Frobenius norm
(Figure~\ref{fig:erank}, Table~\ref{tab:erankw}). In all fourteen cases, our
seven fine-tunes and the seven released pairs, the effective rank of $W$
moves by less than 0.5\%, and the random update moves it by a similar amount:
0.06\% against 0.14\% for Qwen3-8B, 0.010\% against 0.008\% for our SFT run.
This is what Weyl's inequality predicts for an update that is small next to
the weights. An unchanged effective rank therefore follows from the update
being small, and it is equally true of our fine-tunes on Wikipedia and code.
It cannot tell superficial post-training apart from any other fine-tuning.

There is one detail in Timber's favour: on the released models, the real update
moves the effective rank 1.5--4$\times$ less than a random one. Post-training
does change $W$ in a structured way. That is visible only against the random
comparison, and it says the change is structured, not that it is superficial.

\subsection{The cutoff is not special}
\label{sec:cutoff}

\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/cutoff_passk.pdf}
\end{center}
\caption{GSM8K pass@$k$ for Qwen3-0.6B edited as in \citet{wu2025timber}, with
their effective-rank cutoff and with a stable-rank cutoff. Left: first 300
test problems, all five variants. Right: all 1{,}319 problems for the unedited
model and the two $\lambda=0.5$ variants. Eight samples per problem, with the
same sampling seed per problem for every variant.}
\label{fig:cutoff}
\end{figure}

paired bootstrap over problems.}
\label{tab:cutoff}
\begin{center}

\begin{tabular}{lccccc}

\midrule

stable-rank cutoff, $\lambda=0.5$    & 41.4\% & 62.9 & 86.9 & $+0.6$ ($-0.5$, $+1.7$) & $+1.8$ ($+0.2$, $+3.5$) \\

\end{tabular}

Because the effective rank of $\dW$ is close to its noise value (0.79--0.90 of
full rank for the released pairs against 0.87--0.93 for noise), the cutoff it
gives is high. On Qwen3-0.6B it keeps 83\% of the directions and 98.9\% of the
update's energy, so the edit only touches the last 1\%; \citet{wu2025timber}
report 98.82\% for Qwen3-8B. Their own ablation compares truncation at fixed
rank or energy ratios that keep at least 95\% of the energy. We test a much
more aggressive cutoff instead: $\lceil \stablerank(\dW)\cdot r \rceil$ keeps
12\% of the directions and 41\% of the energy, so the tail it scales holds
53$\times$ more energy.

We apply both cutoffs at $\lambda = 0$ (dropping the tail, their Timber-L) and
$\lambda = 0.5$, and score pass@$k$ \citep{chen2021codex} on GSM8K
\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k} in non-thinking mode (temperature 0.7, top-$p$ 0.8,
top-$k$ 20, up to 512 new tokens). GSM8K is not among the benchmarks
\citet{wu2025timber} report, so this tests the cutoff rather than reproducing
their table. Three results stand out (Figure~\ref{fig:cutoff},
Table~\ref{tab:cutoff}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Removing the tail hurts.} Dropping everything past the
  stable-rank cutoff lowers pass@1 by 4.2 points ($-6.9$ to $-1.4$) on the
  first 300 problems, and fewer samples reach a final answer (90.6\% against
  96.5\%). The long tail matters to the model, as
  \citet{ping2024deltacome} also found when truncating updates to low rank.
  \item \textbf{Halving the tail gives the same result with either cutoff.}
  On all 1{,}319 problems the stable-rank variant differs from Timber's by
  $-0.2$ points of pass@1 ($-1.2$ to $+0.9$) and $+1.0$ of pass@8 ($-0.6$ to
  $+2.5$), even though it scales 53$\times$ more of the update.
  \item \textbf{The gain is small on this benchmark.} On the first 300 problems
  Timber's edit raised pass@8 by 3.3 points ($+0.3$ to $+6.3$). On the full
  set the gain is $+0.8$ ($-0.6$ to $+2.3$), and on the 1{,}019 problems
  outside the first 300 it is $+0.1$ ($-1.6$ to $+1.7$).
\end{itemize}

None of this contradicts the gains \citet{wu2025timber} report on other
benchmarks, which we did not test. It does show that the effective-rank cutoff
is not what makes the edit work, and that a pass@$k$ gain measured on a few
hundred problems can shrink a lot on the full set.
