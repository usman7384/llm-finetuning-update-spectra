\section{Case study: is post-training superficial?}
\label{sec:timber}

\citet{wu2025timber} compare released base and instruct models (Llama~3.1 and
3.2 \citep{grattafiori2024llama3}, and Qwen3) through the entropy effective rank of each weight matrix $W$ (not of
$\dW$). They find it almost unchanged by post-training, at about 85\% of full
rank, and read this as weight-level evidence that post-training is
superficial. They also propose an edit, Timber: keep the top
$\lceil \entropyrank(\dW)\rceil$ singular directions of the update and scale
the rest by $\lambda<1$, with $\lambda=0.2$ recommended, which improves Mean@$k$
and pass@$k$ on their benchmarks. We check both with the baselines of
\S\ref{sec:background}.

\subsection{An unchanged effective rank is what any update of this size gives}

\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/random_update.pdf}
\end{center}
\caption{Left: size of the change in the effective rank of the full weight
matrix $W$, $|\Delta\,\entropyrank(W)| / \entropyrank(W)$ averaged over
matrices, for the real base-to-instruct update (blue) and a random update of
the same Frobenius norm (grey). Right: perplexity on held-out
wikitext of Qwen3-0.6B base, unchanged, after a random update of the same size
as its real post-training update (Gaussian, or with the real update's singular
values), and after the real update, which gives the instruct model. The
numbers above the bars are the same size of change in eRank($W$).}
\label{fig:random}
\end{figure}

The measurement reproduces. On Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-8B, two of their pairs,
the effective rank of the base weights is 0.84 and 0.86 of full rank, and
post-training changes it by only 0.13\% and 0.06\%.

But a random update of the same size also leaves it almost unchanged
(Figure~\ref{fig:random}, left). On all seven base/instruct pairs, whose
updates are 1--13\% of the size of the weights, real and random updates both
move the effective rank of $W$ by at most 0.5\%. The real update moves it 1.6
to 3.8 times less than the random one, so post-training does change $W$ in a
structured way, but that is visible only next to the random comparison.

An unchanged effective rank also says nothing about whether the model still
works. Adding to Qwen3-0.6B base a random update of the same size as its real
post-training update, matrix by matrix, moves its effective rank by 0.5\%, but
its GSM8K accuracy falls from 59.5\% to 4--6\%, whether the update is Gaussian
or has exactly the real update's singular values (Table~\ref{tab:damage};
details in Appendix~\ref{app:damage}).
The same random update added to the instruct model takes it from 64.5\% to
11.9\%. The real update, which moves the effective rank even less (0.13\%),
produces the instruct model. So an update can wreck a model or turn it into a
working assistant and leave the effective rank of $W$ almost the same either
way. Perplexity on wikitext rises from 18.1 to 33.1 with either random update
(Figure~\ref{fig:random}, right), but the real update raises it too, to 50.1,
because an instruct model no longer predicts plain web text well. An unchanged effective rank therefore cannot tell a
superficial change from a deep one, or from a destructive one. This does not
show that post-training is deep; it shows that this measurement cannot decide
the question.

\subsection{The edit does not need the effective-rank cutoff}
\label{sec:cutoff}

\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/cutoff_passk.pdf}
\end{center}
\caption{GSM8K pass@$k$ for Qwen3-0.6B (non-thinking mode) with tail edits
and with uniform scaling of the whole update. Eight samples
per problem, the same sampling seed per problem for every variant. Left: first
300 test problems, main variants (every variant is in Table~\ref{tab:cutoff300}). Right: all
1{,}319 (Table~\ref{tab:cutoff}).}
\label{fig:cutoff}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[t]

Differences from the unedited model in points, with 95\% paired bootstrap
intervals over problems, not corrected for multiple comparisons.}
\label{tab:cutoff}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule

\midrule
none                                       & 100\%  & 62.3 & 85.1 & -- & -- \\

tail $\times 0.5$, eRank cutoff (Timber)   & 98.9\% & 63.1 & 85.9 & $+0.7$ ($-0.02$, $+1.6$) & $+0.8$ ($-0.6$, $+2.3$) \\
tail $\times 0.5$, stable-rank cutoff      & 41.4\% & 62.9 & 86.9 & $+0.6$ ($-0.5$, $+1.7$) & $+1.8$ ($+0.2$, $+3.5$) \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{table}

Because the effective rank of $\dW$ sits close to its noise value, the cutoff
it gives is high: on Qwen3-0.6B it keeps 83\% of the directions and 98.9\% of
the update's energy, so the edit touches only the last 1\% (Wu et al.\ report
98.82\% for Qwen3-8B). An edit that small is also fragile in practice: running
the edited model in bf16 distorts it by 16\% of its size at $\lambda=0.2$ and
25\% at $\lambda=0.5$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cutoff}).

We compare edits of the same update on GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}, which is
not among Wu et al.'s benchmarks, using pass@$k$ \citep{chen2021codex}:
Timber's tail edit, the same edit with a stable-rank cutoff that scales 53
times more of the update's energy, and simply scaling the whole update by one
factor per matrix (0.75 on average) so that the edited update has the same
size as the stable-rank edit. We run Timber's edit both at its recommended
$\lambda=0.2$ and at $\lambda=0.5$, and use Qwen3's non-thinking mode, the
mode of Wu et al.'s main table; their thinking-mode results we do not
attempt.

On all 1{,}319 problems, Timber's edit has no detectable effect at either
setting (Table~\ref{tab:cutoff}, Figure~\ref{fig:cutoff}). At $\lambda=0.2$
pass@1 is unchanged ($-0.0$ points, $-0.8$ to $+0.8$) and pass@8 moves by
$+0.2$ ($-1.4$, $+1.8$); at $\lambda=0.5$ the changes are $+0.7$ ($-0.02$,
$+1.6$) and $+0.8$ ($-0.6$, $+2.3$). The stable-rank edit gives a similar
pass@1 and a pass@8 gain of $+1.8$ ($+0.2$, $+3.5$).

Simply scaling the whole update does better than either tail edit: pass@1
rises by $+3.4$ points ($+2.4$, $+4.5$) and pass@8 by $+2.7$ ($+1.1$,
$+4.2$), with nearly the same share of answered samples (96.4\% against
97.4\%). It makes the update as small as the stable-rank edit does, but evenly
from every direction instead of from the tail. So on this benchmark the gain
comes from making the update smaller, and choosing which directions to shrink
by a spectral cutoff, whether eRank or stable rank, adds nothing. Task
arithmetic already treats the scale of an update as a knob worth tuning
\citep{ilharco2023task}; here that knob alone beats the spectral edits.

On the first 300 problems we also ran the extreme $\lambda=0$, which removes
the tail completely (Table~\ref{tab:cutoff300}). Removing Timber's tail, 1.1\%
of the update's energy, changes nothing measurable ($+0.5$ points of pass@1).
Removing the stable-rank ``tail'', which is 59\% of the update's energy and so
most of the update, costs 4.2 points against the unedited model and 5.3 against
halving it. Removing most of an update hurts; this says little about the
weakest directions alone. A uniform scaling matched in size to Timber's edit is
almost erased by bf16 storage (88\% of the change is lost) and, like Timber's
edit, has no clear effect ($+0.8$, interval $-0.8$ to $+2.4$).

None of this tests the gains Wu et al.\ report on their benchmarks with their
settings. It shows that, on GSM8K in non-thinking mode, the effective-rank
cutoff is not what makes an edit of this kind work.
