\section{Case study: is post-training superficial?}
\label{sec:timber}

\citet{wu2025timber} compare released base and instruct models (Llama~3 and
Qwen3) through the entropy effective rank of each weight matrix. They find it
almost unchanged by post-training, at about 85\% of full rank, and read this
as weight-level evidence that post-training is superficial. They also propose
an edit: keep the top $\lceil \entropyrank(\dW)\cdot r\rceil$ singular
directions of the update and scale the rest by $\lambda<1$, which improves
pass@$k$ on their benchmarks. We check both with the baselines of
\S\ref{sec:background}.

\subsection{An unchanged effective rank is what any update of this size gives}

\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/random_update.pdf}
\end{center}
\caption{Left: change in the effective rank of the full weight matrix $W$ for
the real base-to-instruct update (blue) and a random update of the same
Frobenius norm (grey), averaged over matrices. Right: perplexity on held-out
wikitext of Qwen3-0.6B base after a random update of the same size as its real
post-training update, Gaussian or with the real update's singular values.}
\label{fig:random}
\end{figure}

The measurement reproduces. On Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-8B, two of their pairs,
the effective rank of the base weights is 0.84 and 0.86 of full rank, and
post-training changes it by only 0.13\% and 0.06\%.

But a random update of the same size leaves it just as unchanged
(Figure~\ref{fig:random}, left). On all seven base/instruct pairs, real and
random updates move the effective rank of $W$ by less than 0.5\%, as Weyl's
inequality suggests for updates much smaller than the weights. On the released
models the real update moves it 1.5--4 times less than the random one, so
post-training does change $W$ in a structured way, but that is visible only
next to the random comparison.

The random updates are not harmless. Adding to Qwen3-0.6B base a random update
of the same size as its real post-training update, matrix by matrix, raises
its perplexity on held-out wikitext from 18.1 to 33.1, whether the update is
Gaussian or has exactly the real update's singular values
(Figure~\ref{fig:random}, right), while its effective rank moves by 0.5\%. An
unchanged effective rank therefore cannot tell a superficial change from a
damaging one, or from fine-tuning on Wikipedia (\S\ref{sec:controls}). This
does not show that post-training is deep; it shows that this measurement
cannot decide the question.

\subsection{The edit does not need the effective-rank cutoff}
\label{sec:cutoff}

\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/cutoff_passk.pdf}

Timber-style tail edits and with uniform scaling of the whole update. Eight
samples per problem, the same sampling seed per problem for every variant.
Left: first 300 test problems. Right: all 1{,}319.}
\label{fig:cutoff}
\end{figure}

Because the effective rank of $\dW$ sits close to its noise value, the cutoff
it gives is high: on Qwen3-0.6B it keeps 83\% of the directions and 98.9\% of
the update's energy, so the edit touches only the last 1\% (Wu et al.\ report
98.82\% for Qwen3-8B). An edit that small is also fragile in practice: storing
the edited model in bf16 distorts it by about a quarter.

We compare three edits of the same update on GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k},
which is not among Wu et al.'s benchmarks, using pass@$k$
\citep{chen2021codex}: Timber's tail edit, the same edit with a stable-rank
cutoff that scales 53 times more of the update's energy, and simply scaling the
whole update by 0.75 so that the edited update has the same size as the
stable-rank edit. Removing the tail entirely instead of halving it costs 4.2
points of pass@1 (Appendix~\ref{app:cutoff}), so the long tail matters to the
model.

XX: full-set result for uniform scaling, and what it implies.

None of this tests the gains Wu et al.\ report on other benchmarks. It shows
that, on this benchmark, the effective-rank cutoff is not what makes the edit
work.
