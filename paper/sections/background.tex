\section{Measuring the rank of an update}
\label{sec:background}

Let $\dW \in \R^{m\times n}$ be the update to one weight matrix,
$r = \min(m,n)$, and $\sigma_1 \geq \dots \geq \sigma_r$ its singular values.
Write $p_i = \sigma_i^2 / \sum_j \sigma_j^2$ for the share of the update's
energy (squared Frobenius norm) in direction $i$. We report every measure as a
fraction of $r$, so that matrices of different shapes can be averaged.

\paragraph{Five measures.} In plain words, each asks ``how many directions
does this update really use?'', and they differ in how much the strongest
directions count.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Threshold count} $\thresholdrank$: how many $\sigma_i$ are at
  least $0.01\,\sigma_1$. We use it as the simplest example of counting; the
  numerical rank reported by \citet{mukherjee2025rl} counts in the same way,
  with a tolerance they do not state (by default it is set by floating-point
  precision).
  \item \textbf{Energy rank} $\energyrank$: the smallest $k$ with
  $\sum_{i\leq k} p_i \geq 0.9$, the measure of \citet{biderman2024lora}.
  \item \textbf{Entropy effective rank} $\entropyrank$
  \citep{roy2007effective}: $\exp\bigl(-\sum_i q_i \log q_i\bigr)$ with
  $q_i = \sigma_i/\sum_j \sigma_j$, as used by \citet{wu2025timber}.
  \item \textbf{Participation ratio}: $1/\sum_i p_i^2$.
  \item \textbf{Stable rank} $\stablerank$ \citep{rudelson2007sampling}:
  $\|\dW\|_F^2/\|\dW\|_2^2 = 1/p_1$.
\end{itemize}
All five ignore the overall scale of $\dW$, which matters because the updates
we compare differ in size by orders of magnitude.

\paragraph{How the measures relate.} Three of the five are effective ranks of
one kind: the exponential of a R\'enyi entropy of $p$ of order $\alpha$,
$R_\alpha = \bigl(\sum_i p_i^\alpha\bigr)^{1/(1-\alpha)}$. Order 0 counts the
nonzero directions (the threshold count is a thresholded version of it),
order 2 is the participation ratio, and order $\infty$ is the stable rank.
The higher the order, the more the top directions dominate. This is why the
measures can disagree about the same matrix: a spectrum with a few strong
directions and a long tail of weak ones has many directions to count but most
of its weight on top. The other two are close relatives rather than members:
eRank uses the Shannon entropy of $q \propto \sigma$ instead of
$p \propto \sigma^2$, and the energy rank counts directions up to a fixed share
of $p$. We list them between the threshold count and the participation ratio,
which is where their values fall for our updates.

Note that $R_\alpha$ can only fall as $\alpha$ grows, for any matrix, so the
order of the raw values tells us nothing. What does carry information is how
far each measure puts an update below noise.

\paragraph{The gap to noise.} For each measure we report
$1 - \bar{x}_{\text{update}}/\bar{x}_{\text{noise}}$, where $\bar{x}$ is the
mean of the measure over all matrices of a model: 0 means the update looks
like noise, values near 1 mean it is far more concentrated. Intervals come
from a bootstrap over layers (\S\ref{sec:setup}). The measures have different
ceilings (the threshold count of noise is already 0.996), so the gap is best
compared across updates for one measure, and only roughly across measures.

\paragraph{The noise baseline.} A value of any of these measures only means
something next to its value for a matrix with no structure. For our own runs
the baseline is the difference between two independent random initialisations
of the same model; for released models, one Gaussian draw for each matrix,
with the same shape. The spectrum of such a matrix follows the
Marchenko--Pastur law, whose upper edge (the Bai--Yin limit,
\citealp{bai2010spectral}) gives a stable rank close to
$mn/(\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n})^2$, a quarter of $r$ for square matrices. Our
random-init pairs match this to within 0.01 for every matrix shape of
Qwen2.5-0.5B (Table~\ref{tab:mp} in Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}), and the
two baselines agree: on Qwen2.5-0.5B the stable rank is 0.436 for the
random-init pair and 0.434 for the Gaussian matrices. A Gaussian matrix is the least
structured baseline possible, so as a second reference we also measure the
pretrained weights $W$ themselves.

\paragraph{The rounding floor.} Released checkpoints are usually stored in
bf16, which rounds every weight to 8 significant bits. The rounding adds a
small noise matrix to $\dW$. For each released pair we simulate it (uniform
error up to half a bf16 unit at each weight, independently for both
checkpoints) and ask how much of a measure it could account for. This is an
upper bound: when a weight barely moves, its two rounding errors are not
independent, and the stored difference is exactly zero. So we also report the
direct evidence, the share of entries of $\dW$ that are exactly zero.

\paragraph{Measures of $W$ rather than $\dW$.} Some studies measure the full
weight matrix before and after fine-tuning. By Weyl's inequality
\citep{horn2012matrix}, each singular value of $W + \dW$ is within
$\|\dW\|_2$ of the corresponding one of $W$. When the update is small
compared with $W$, the large singular values of $W$ barely move; the small
ones can move more relative to their size, so the bound alone does not settle
how much a measure of $W$ can change. We therefore test it directly against a
random update of the same size, $W + G\,\|\dW\|_F/\|G\|_F$ with $G$
Gaussian.
