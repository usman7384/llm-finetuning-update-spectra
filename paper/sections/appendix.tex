Training a run took 1.3--3.7 hours on one GPU, and 3.6--8.6 hours for DPO
(Table~\ref{tab:runs}). Held-out reward accuracy, on about 190 validation
pairs that are never swapped, is 65\% and 74\% for the two DPO seeds and 51\%
for the swapped control. The base
tokenizer of SmolLM2-360M has no chat template, so SFT borrows the one from
SmolLM2-360M-Instruct. Second seeds change both the training seed and the data
sample. Every run records its exact training rows, token count and the
fraction of unchanged weights. Dropping the top direction leaves every update
far below noise (0.440 for Qwen, 0.357 for SmolLM2), so stable rank is not
driven by a single outlying direction.

We tested one possible explanation: that the shuffled-words update mainly
shifts the output towards frequent tokens. For each layer's
\texttt{down\_proj} update on Qwen, we map the top left singular vector to the
vocabulary through the final norm and the tied unembedding (ignoring the
layers in between, as the logit lens does) and correlate the resulting logit
shift with the log frequency of each token in the run's training data,
against 50 random directions. In the last third of the network the top
direction of several runs lines up with frequency, not only shuffled words:
the correlation beats the random 95th percentile (about 0.19) in layers 18--23
for shuffled words, in most of layers 15--23 for SFT and code, and in layer 23
for wikitext. The mean correlation over the last third is 0.33 for shuffled
words, 0.25 for SFT, 0.19 for code and 0.09 for wikitext. With 24 layers and
four runs, and no correction for multiple tests, we read this only as a
pattern: frequency alignment in late layers is common to fine-tuning, so it
does not explain why the shuffled-words update is the most concentrated.

\section{Random-update details}
\label{app:damage}

Each random update is drawn matrix by matrix with its own seed and scaled to
the Frobenius norm of the real update of that matrix; embeddings and norms are
left alone. The edit is made in fp32 and the model then runs in bf16. Answers
are greedy, up to 256 new tokens for the base model (8-shot prompt from the
first eight GSM8K training problems, answer after ``The answer is'') and 512
for the instruct model (chat template, thinking off, answer in
\verb|\boxed{}|). The randomly updated models fail rather than run out of
tokens: on the answers that finished within the limit, the base model with a
Gaussian update or with the real spectrum on random directions is right 7\%
of the time, against 62\% for the unedited base.

We also scored the base model plus its real update with the base model's
8-shot prompt. It reaches only 19.9\%, but not because the update damages the
model: post-trained weights write long chat-style reasoning in that format, and
59\% of their answers hit the 256-token limit (on the rest they are right 42\%
of the time). This combination also keeps the base model's embeddings, which
post-training changed by 13\%. The fair test of the real update is the
instruct model in its own format (Table~\ref{tab:damage}).

\section{Edit experiment details}
\label{app:cutoff}

The prompt is the GSM8K question followed by ``Please reason step by step, and
put your final answer within \verb|\boxed{}|.'', in Qwen3's chat template with
thinking turned off, sampled at temperature 0.7, top-$p$ 0.8 and top-$k$ 20 for
up to 512 new tokens. The answer is the last \verb|\boxed{}| expression, or the
last number if there is none. The edit is applied to every attention and MLP
projection in fp32 and the model then runs in bf16. Rounding then distorts the
edit: the error $\|\mathrm{bf16}(W') - W'\|_F$, where $W'$ is the edited
matrix, is on average 25\% (16\% at $\lambda=0.2$) of the intended change
$\|W' - W\|_F$ for Timber's
tail edit, 4\% for the stable-rank tail edit, 5.5\% for uniform scaling
matched to the stable-rank edit and 88\% for uniform scaling matched to
Timber's edit, which bf16 storage almost erases. The uniform scaling factor is
computed per matrix; its mean is 0.75 for the stable-rank match. pass@$k$ uses
the unbiased estimator of
\citet{chen2021codex} with $n=8$.
