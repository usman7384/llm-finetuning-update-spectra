\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When a language model is fine-tuned, each weight matrix $W$ changes by some
update $\dW$. A natural way to ask what fine-tuning did is to ask how many
independent directions $\dW$ uses, that is, its rank. A low-rank update
suggests a small, targeted change; a full-rank one suggests a diffuse change.

The question matters most for alignment. The superficial alignment hypothesis
\citep{zhou2023lima} holds that alignment mostly teaches a model which style
to answer in, and output-level evidence supports it \citep{lin2024urial}. If
that is true, one might expect alignment to leave a small, structured
footprint in the weights, and recent work has looked for one in the rank of
$\dW$ \citep{wu2025timber}.

Real updates are full rank in the strict sense, so studies use softer
measures: how many singular values exceed 1\% of the largest, how many
directions hold 90\% of the energy \citep{biderman2024lora}, or the entropy
effective rank \citep{roy2007effective, wu2025timber}. We began this project by
measuring SFT and DPO updates of a small model with the first of these and got
a puzzling answer. Our SFT update scored 99.1\% of full rank. The difference
between two random initialisations of the same model, which is pure noise,
scored 99.6\%. By that measure, fine-tuning looks like noise.

It is not. Stable rank \citep{rudelson2007sampling}, computed from the same
singular values, says the SFT update is three times more concentrated than
noise, and its top direction holds eight times as much of the energy. The two
answers differ because the measures look at different parts of the spectrum,
and the counting measures had never been compared with what noise gives.

This paper makes that comparison systematically, on our own fine-tunes of two
small models and on seven released base/instruct pairs up to 8B parameters.
We find:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Counting measures make updates look like noise.} They put real
  updates only 0.4--31\% below a random matrix of the same shape, while stable
  rank puts them 1.6--9$\times$ below (\S\ref{sec:metrics}).
  \item \textbf{Updates have a few strong directions and a long tail.} Most of
  the energy sits in a slowly decaying tail, which the counting measures
  count; the strong directions are what stable rank sees. The pretrained
  weights have a similar shape (\S\ref{sec:headtail}).
  \item \textbf{This is about fine-tuning, not alignment.} Fine-tunes on
  Wikipedia text and Python code, matched in tokens, are as concentrated as
  instruction tuning (\S\ref{sec:controls}).
  \item \textbf{It holds for released models up to 8B} (\S\ref{sec:released}).
  \item \textbf{It changes how earlier evidence reads.} \citet{wu2025timber}
  take an unchanged effective rank of the weights as evidence that
  post-training is superficial. A random update of the same size leaves it
  just as unchanged (\S\ref{sec:timber}).
\end{enumerate}

The practical message is short: report rank measures of weight updates next to
the same measure on noise, and test claims about alignment against
fine-tuning without alignment content. Behavioural results from the same runs
(evaluations, a LoRA rank sweep and DPO) are in Appendix~\ref{app:behavior}.
