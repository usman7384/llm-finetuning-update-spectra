\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When a language model is fine-tuned, each weight matrix $W$ changes by an
update $\dW$. A natural way to ask what fine-tuning did is to ask how many
independent directions $\dW$ uses, that is, its rank. A low-rank update
suggests a small, targeted change; a full-rank one suggests a diffuse change.

The question matters most for alignment. The superficial alignment hypothesis
\citep{zhou2023lima} holds that alignment mostly teaches a model which style
to answer in, and output-level evidence supports it \citep{lin2024urial}. If
that is true, one might expect alignment to leave a small, structured
footprint in the weights, and recent work has looked for one in the rank of
$\dW$ and of $W$ \citep{wu2025timber}. Related work reports that fine-tuning
updates are ``high rank'' \citep{biderman2024lora} or ``nearly full-rank''
\citep{mukherjee2025rl}.

Real updates are full rank in the strict sense, so these studies use softer
measures: how many singular values exceed 1\% of the largest, how many
directions hold 90\% of the energy, or the entropy effective rank
\citep{roy2007effective}. We began this project by measuring an SFT update
with the first of these and got a puzzling answer. The update scored 97\% of
full rank. The difference between two random initialisations of the same
model, which is pure noise, scored 99.6\%. By that measure, fine-tuning looks
almost like noise.

It is not. Stable rank \citep{rudelson2007sampling}, computed from the same
singular values, puts the update 4.5 times below noise, and its top direction
holds 6.6 times as much of the energy. The two answers differ because the
measures weigh different parts of the spectrum, and the counting measures are
rarely compared with what noise gives. Comparing against noise is an old idea
in statistics \citep{horn1965parallel, gavish2014optimal} and has been used to
study trained weights \citep{martin2021implicit, thamm2022random}; this paper
applies it to fine-tuning updates.

Our findings:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Counting measures make updates look like noise.} They put our
  updates only 2--36\% below a random matrix of the same shape; stable rank
  puts them 78--94\% below. All the measures belong to one family, and the gap
  grows with how much weight a measure gives to the top directions
  (\S\ref{sec:metrics}).
  \item \textbf{Updates have a few strong directions and a long tail}, and are
  more concentrated than the pretrained weights they are added to
  (\S\ref{sec:headtail}).
  \item \textbf{The shape comes from fine-tuning, not from alignment.} With the
  amount of training held fixed, instruction tuning, Wikipedia and Python code
  give almost the same update, and text with its word order scrambled gives the
  most concentrated update of all (\S\ref{sec:controls}).
  \item \textbf{It holds for released models}, fourteen base/instruct and
  base/specialist pairs up to 8B parameters. On their bf16 checkpoints, much of
  what the threshold count counts could come from rounding alone
  (\S\ref{sec:released}).
  \item \textbf{It changes how earlier evidence reads.} An unchanged effective
  rank of $W$ after post-training, read by \citet{wu2025timber} as a sign that
  post-training is superficial, is what any update of that size produces,
  including random updates that clearly damage the model (\S\ref{sec:timber}).
\end{enumerate}

The practical message is short: report rank measures of weight updates next to
the same measure on noise, and test claims about alignment against fine-tuning
without alignment content.
