\section{What the rank measures say}
\label{sec:results}

Table~\ref{tab:measures} gives the five measures for our updates. By the
threshold count, the Qwen SFT, wikitext and code updates are at 97\% of full
rank, close to the 99.6\% of noise. Stable rank tells a different story: every
update is several times below noise, 4.5 times for the Qwen SFT update, 10 to
17 times for the SmolLM2 updates, and up to 39 times for shuffled words.

Figure~\ref{fig:gaps} shows how the measures fit together. Ordered from the
threshold count to stable rank, each measure puts the updates further below
noise: about 2\% for the threshold count, 12\% for eRank, 25\% for the 90\%
energy rank, 50\% for the participation ratio and 78\% for stable rank on
Qwen, and the same order on SmolLM2. The values for noise fall along the same
order, and what grows is the distance between update and noise. Along the
whole family of R\'enyi ranks the gap grows steadily with the order, then
levels off (Figure~\ref{fig:renyi} in Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}). This is
expected for any spectrum with a heavier head than noise, so we read it less
as a finding than as a warning: the answer depends on which order one
happens to pick. Every interval excludes zero, so all five measures do detect
that the updates are not random. The problem is the size of the gap. The two
measures used in the literature, eRank and the energy rank, put the SFT,
wikitext, code and DPO updates 12--18\% and 24--35\% below noise; stable rank
puts them 78--94\% below. The threshold count is also arbitrary: at
$0.1\,\sigma_1$ instead of $0.01\,\sigma_1$ the Qwen SFT update sits 18\%
below noise instead of 2\% (Table~\ref{tab:threshold}). A reader who sees
``97\% of full rank'' without the noise value next to it will conclude that the
update is diffuse, which is what we concluded at first. The same holds for
every matrix type (Table~\ref{tab:pertype}).

\subsection{A few strong directions and a long tail}
\label{sec:headtail}

\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/spectrum_head_tail.pdf}
\end{center}
\caption{Where the energy sits, averaged over the 168 matrices of
Qwen2.5-0.5B. Left: share of energy in the top fraction of directions. Right:
singular values relative to the largest. Updates put much more energy in their
top directions than noise does, then decay slowly over a long tail. The
pretrained weights (dashed) sit between the updates and noise.}
\label{fig:spectrum}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum} shows why the measures disagree. At the top of the
spectrum the updates are far more concentrated than noise: the top direction of
the Qwen SFT update holds 4.2\% of its energy against 0.6\% for noise. Below
that the spectrum decays slowly, and this long tail still holds most of the
energy. Stable rank is dominated by the top, so it sees the concentration; the
counting measures count directions, most of which are in the tail, so they see
a nearly full-rank matrix. Both descriptions are true of the same matrix:
fine-tuning updates are high rank \citep{biderman2024lora} and they are
concentrated. Long-tailed update spectra have been noted before, in work on
compressing updates \citep{ping2024deltacome}; what the noise baseline adds is
how far from random the head of the spectrum is. Nor is the result driven by
one outlying direction: without the top direction, stable rank is 0.126 for
the Qwen SFT update against 0.440 for noise (Table~\ref{tab:runs}).

The pretrained weights are a harder reference than noise, since they are
themselves far from random, and the updates are more concentrated than them
too, on average. The stable rank of the pretrained weights is 0.178 on Qwen
and 0.140 on SmolLM2, against 0.09--0.10 and 0.020--0.034 for the SFT,
wikitext and code updates: 46--48\% below the weights on Qwen and 75--86\% on
SmolLM2, with layer-bootstrap intervals well clear of zero. This does not hold
for every matrix type: on Qwen, for \texttt{down\_proj}, the SFT update and the
weights are level (Table~\ref{tab:pertype}). The threshold count cannot see
the difference either: it puts the Qwen updates and the pretrained weights at
the same 97--98\%.

\label{sec:controls}

A concentrated SFT update says nothing about instruction tuning unless
fine-tuning on other data looks different. With the model, optimizer and
number of training tokens held fixed, SFT does not stand out. On Qwen, SFT,
wikitext and code give stable ranks of 0.097, 0.093 and 0.094; every paired
difference between them is under 0.005 and its interval includes zero
(Table~\ref{tab:paired}), and a second seed moves each by at most 0.0014. On
SmolLM2 the data does leave a mark: code is the least concentrated (0.034),
wikitext the most (0.020) and SFT sits between them (0.025). These
differences are real, not seed noise: a second seed of each SmolLM2 run moves
its stable rank by at most 0.0003. Nor are they an artefact of the code runs
taking 2.6--2.7 times fewer optimizer steps than SFT (\S\ref{sec:setup}): training on
code for as many steps as SFT, which is 2.5--2.7 times more code tokens, moves the
code update further from SFT, not closer, on both models (stable rank 0.114
on Qwen, 0.042 on SmolLM2). So what the data teaches, and how much of it, does
shape the update. What it does not do is single out instruction tuning: in
both models SFT does not stand out from the ordinary language-model runs.

Similar stable rank means a similar spectral shape, not the same update, so we
also compared the directions. For each pair of runs we measured the overlap of
the top-16 left singular subspaces of each matrix's update, averaged over
matrices (1 would be identical subspaces; two random subspaces give 0.02--0.04;
Appendix~\ref{app:overlap}). Two runs on the same kind of data with different
seeds overlap by 0.26--0.32 on Qwen and 0.34--0.51 on SmolLM2. Runs on
different data overlap less, 0.20--0.22 on Qwen and 0.12--0.13 on SmolLM2, but
still four to six times more than random. So the data steers which directions
an update uses, more than it changes the update's shape.

Preference training gives the same answer. Starting from the Qwen SFT model,
we ran DPO \citep{rafailov2023dpo} on 10k UltraFeedback pairs
\citep{cui2024ultrafeedback}, and a control on the same pairs with chosen and
rejected swapped in a random half of them, in the spirit of random-label
controls \citep{zhang2017rethinking}. The prompts, responses and amount of
training are identical, but there is no net preference to learn; the control
does share the unswapped half of its pairs with the DPO run, so it removes the
preference signal, not everything the two runs see in common
(Appendix~\ref{app:training}). On about 190 held-out pairs the DPO model
prefers the chosen answer 65\% of the time (74\% for a second seed); the
control is at chance, 51\%. Yet the two updates are alike on every measure
(Table~\ref{tab:measures}): stable rank 0.053 for DPO and 0.055 for the
control, against 0.436 for noise, and the same size. The paired difference,
$+0.002$, is larger than the gap between the two DPO seeds ($+0.001$) but
under 4\% of the value and about $1/200$ of the distance to noise
(Table~\ref{tab:paired}). The directions agree too: the real DPO update
overlaps with the swapped control (0.27) almost exactly as much as with a
second seed of itself (0.28). Like the SFT update, the DPO update is more
concentrated than the pretrained weights and far more than noise, and at the
strength of preference learning we reach, almost none of that, in shape or in
direction, comes from having learned the preferences.

The surprise is the shuffled-words control. Text with its word order
scrambled, which has little to learn beyond word frequencies, gives the most
concentrated update on both models: stable rank 0.042 on Qwen and 0.009 on
SmolLM2, with 9\% and 21\% of the energy in a single direction. This holds in
every quarter of the network (Table~\ref{tab:shuffled}). The model fits this
data poorly (training loss 5.4, against 2.2 for wikitext;
Table~\ref{tab:runs}), and we do not know why that gives a more concentrated
update. One explanation we tested, that the update mainly pushes the output
towards frequent tokens, does not single it out: the top directions of the
SFT and code updates line up with token frequency in late layers too
(Appendix~\ref{app:shuffled}). We report it as an observation: the
concentration of a fine-tuning update depends on the training process and the
data, not on whether the data teach instruction following or preferences.

Released instruct models go through much larger and more varied
post-training than our runs. The same pattern holds for all thirteen pairs
(Figure~\ref{fig:released}, Table~\ref{tab:released}). The threshold count
reads 91--99\%, close to noise; stable rank puts every update 1.6 to 27 times
below noise, and dropping the top direction changes little, so no single
outlier drives it. Every update also has tens to hundreds of directions above
the largest singular value a same-size Gaussian matrix would have
(Table~\ref{tab:released_more}).

For most pairs, bf16 rounding is not a concern: 91--100\% of their
directions are stronger than anything rounding alone can produce. For three
it is. 14\% of the entries of the Qwen2.5-1.5B update, and 39\% and 52\% of
the OLMo DPO and RL updates, are exactly zero, and only 78\%, 13\% and 3\% of
their directions rise above the simulated rounding floor
(Table~\ref{tab:released_more}). For these updates much of what the threshold
count counts may be rounding. Rounding adds many tiny singular values, which
stable rank largely ignores.

The OLMo 2 stages show where post-training changes the weights. Almost all of
the change is in SFT: its update is 9.8\% of the size of the weights, has
stable rank 0.080 against 0.336 for noise, and the whole base-to-instruct
update has the same size and shape to within 0.001. The DPO and RL stages are
tiny, under half a percent of the weights, and only 13\% and 3\% of their
directions rise above the simulated rounding floor. Their very low stable
rank therefore reflects a few real directions on top of rounding, and we draw
no conclusion about preference training from them; our own DPO runs
(\S\ref{sec:controls}) are the controlled test.

The specialists give a mixed picture. Against an instruct release of the same
base, Qwen2.5-Coder-0.5B (stable rank 0.194) is more concentrated than
Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (0.280), while Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B and
Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B (0.200 and 0.191) are less concentrated than
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (0.051). The specialists differ from the instruct models
in both content and amount of training (their updates are about as large as
the weights themselves), so this is not a controlled comparison; our matched
runs in \S\ref{sec:controls} are. What the released models show is that
concentration varies with how much and what kind of training was done, and
that nothing in it sets instruct updates apart.
