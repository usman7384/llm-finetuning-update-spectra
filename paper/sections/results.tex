Table~\ref{tab:measures} gives the five measures for our updates. By the
threshold count, every Qwen update is at 97\% of full rank, close to the 99.6\%
of noise. Stable rank tells a different story: every update is several times
below noise, 4.5 times for the Qwen SFT update, 10 to 17 times for the SmolLM2
updates, and up to 39 times for shuffled words.

Figure~\ref{fig:gaps} shows how the measures fit together. Ordered from the
threshold count to stable rank, each measure puts the updates further below
noise: about 2\% for the threshold count, 12\% for eRank, 25\% for the 90\%
energy rank, 50\% for the participation ratio and 78\% for stable rank on
Qwen, and the same order on SmolLM2. This is the R\'enyi ordering of
\S\ref{sec:background}. Every interval excludes zero, so all five measures do
detect that the updates are not random. The problem is size: a reader who sees
``97\% of full rank'' without the noise value next to it will conclude that the
update is diffuse, which is what we concluded at first. The same holds for
every matrix type (Appendix~\ref{app:pertype}).

\subsection{A few strong directions and a long tail}
\label{sec:headtail}

\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/spectrum_head_tail.pdf}
\end{center}
\caption{Where the energy sits, averaged over the 168 matrices of
Qwen2.5-0.5B. Left: share of energy in the top fraction of directions. Right:
singular values relative to the largest. Updates put much more energy in their
top directions than noise does, then decay slowly over a long tail. The
pretrained weights (dashed) sit between the updates and noise.}
\label{fig:spectrum}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum} shows why the measures disagree. At the top of the
spectrum the updates are far more concentrated than noise: the top direction of
the Qwen SFT update holds 4.2\% of its energy against 0.6\% for noise. Below
that the spectrum decays slowly, and this long tail still holds most of the
energy. Stable rank is dominated by the top, so it sees the concentration; the
counting measures count directions, most of which are in the tail, so they see
a nearly full-rank matrix. Both descriptions are true of the same matrix:
fine-tuning updates are high rank \citep{biderman2024lora} and they are
concentrated.

The updates are also more concentrated than the weights they are added to.
The stable rank of the pretrained weights is 0.178 on Qwen and 0.140 on
SmolLM2, against 0.09--0.10 and 0.020--0.034 for the SFT, wikitext and code
updates. The threshold count cannot see this either: it puts the Qwen updates
and the pretrained weights at the same 97--98\%.

\subsection{The shape comes from fine-tuning, not from alignment}
\label{sec:controls}

A concentrated SFT update says nothing about alignment unless fine-tuning on
other data looks different. With the model, optimizer and amount of training
held fixed, it does not. On Qwen, SFT, wikitext and code give stable ranks of
0.097, 0.093 and 0.094, and the differences between them cannot be told apart
from zero (Table~\ref{tab:paired}); a second seed moves each by at most 0.0014.
On SmolLM2 the three are again close (0.020--0.034), with small differences
that do clear zero. Instruction data leaves no special mark.

The surprise is the shuffled-words control. Text with its word order
scrambled, which has nothing to learn but word frequencies, gives the most
concentrated update on both models: stable rank 0.042 on Qwen and 0.009 on
SmolLM2, with 9\% and 21\% of the energy in a single direction. This holds in
every quarter of the network (Appendix~\ref{app:shuffled}). In the last layers
the top direction of this update pushes the output towards frequent tokens,
but in earlier layers it does not, so word frequency explains only part of it.
We report it as an observation: the concentration of a fine-tuning update
depends on the training process and the data, not on whether the data teach
alignment.

Released instruct models go through much larger and more varied
post-training than our runs. The same pattern holds for all of them
(Figure~\ref{fig:released}, Table~\ref{tab:released}). The threshold count
reads 91--99\%, close to noise; stable rank puts every update 1.6 to 28 times
below noise, and dropping the top direction changes little, so no single
outlier drives it. Every update also has tens to hundreds of directions above
the largest singular value a same-size Gaussian matrix would have
(Appendix~\ref{app:released}).

Rounding gives a second reason to distrust the threshold count. For several
pairs, simulated bf16 rounding alone could produce most of what it counts: 75\%
for Qwen2.5-0.5B and 92\% for Qwen2.5-1.5B. Rounding adds many tiny singular
values, which stable rank largely ignores.

The OLMo 2 stages show where the change happens. Almost all of it is in SFT:
the base-to-instruct update has the same shape as the SFT stage alone. The DPO
and RL stages are so small that only 13\% and 3\% of their directions rise
above the rounding floor, so their very low stable rank reflects a few real
directions on top of rounding noise, and we draw no further conclusion from
them.

One comparison needs care. The math and code specialists, trained on far more
data, are less concentrated (stable rank 0.19--0.20) than the instruct
releases (0.04--0.28). This is not a controlled comparison, since the
specialists differ in both content and amount of training; our matched runs in
\S\ref{sec:controls} are the controlled test, and there the content made no
difference. What the released models show is that concentration varies with
how much and what kind of training was done, so it cannot serve as a signature
of alignment.
