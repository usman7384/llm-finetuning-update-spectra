\begin{abstract}
Studies of what fine-tuning does to a language model often summarise the
weight update $\dW$ by its rank. We compare the rank measures used for this
against the same measures on a random matrix of the same shape, and against
two harder references: the pretrained weights, and fine-tunes without
alignment content. Measures that count directions put our fine-tuning
updates only a little below noise; stable rank, computed from the same
singular values, puts them far below. Both are right: an update has a few
strong directions on top of a long tail, and the gap to noise grows with how
much weight a measure gives to the top. The pattern holds for thirteen
released pairs up to 8B parameters. It is not a mark of alignment: with the
amount of training held fixed, instruction data does not stand out from
Wikipedia or Python code; DPO on one model gives the same update whether its
preference labels are real or half swapped at random, at the modest strength
of preference learning we reach; and scrambled text gives the most
concentrated update of all. Applied to a recent claim that post-training is
superficial because the effective rank of the weights barely changes, the
baselines show that a random update of the same size leaves that rank almost
unchanged too, while cutting GSM8K accuracy from 60\% to 5\%. An edit built
on the effective rank of the update has no measurable effect on GSM8K or,
within about three points, on GPQA-Diamond in non-thinking mode, while simply
shrinking the whole update helps on GSM8K. Rank measures of weight updates
need a noise baseline, and claims about alignment need a control without
alignment content.
\end{abstract}
