\begin{abstract}
Studies of what fine-tuning does to a language model often summarise the
weight update $\dW$ by its rank. We compare the rank measures used for this
against the same measures on random matrices of the same shape. Measures that
count directions (a threshold count of singular values, the number of
directions holding 90\% of the energy, and the entropy effective rank) put our
fine-tuning updates only 2--36\% below noise, so on their own they make
fine-tuning look unstructured. Stable rank puts the same updates 78--94\%
below noise: a few strong directions on top of a long tail. These measures form one family,
and the gap grows steadily with how much weight a measure gives to the top
directions. The pattern holds for full fine-tunes of two small models and for
fourteen released base/instruct and base/specialist pairs up to 8B
parameters. The concentration is not a sign of alignment: with the amount of
training held fixed, instruction tuning, Wikipedia and Python code give
almost the same update, and scrambled text gives the most concentrated update
of all. Applying the same baselines to a recent claim that post-training is
superficial because the effective rank of the weights barely changes, we find
that a random update of the same size leaves it just as unchanged while
clearly damaging the model. Rank measures of weight updates should be
reported against a noise baseline.
\end{abstract}
