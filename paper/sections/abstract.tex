\begin{abstract}
Studies of what fine-tuning does to a language model often summarise the
weight update $\dW$ by its rank. We compare the rank measures used for this
against the same measures on a random matrix of the same shape, and against
two harder references: the pretrained weights, and fine-tunes without
alignment content. Measures that count directions put our fine-tuning
updates only a little below noise; stable rank, computed from the same
singular values, puts them far below. Both are right: an update has a few
strong directions on top of a long tail. The pattern holds for thirteen
released pairs up to 8B parameters, and it is not a mark of alignment: with
the amount of training held fixed, instruction data does not stand out from
Wikipedia or code; DPO on one model gives the same update whether its
preference labels are real or half swapped at random, at the modest
preference strength we reach; and scrambled text gives the most concentrated
update of all. Applied to a recent claim that post-training is superficial
because the effective rank of the weights barely changes, the baselines show
that a random update of the same size leaves that rank almost unchanged
while cutting GSM8K accuracy from 60\% to 5\%, and that an edit built on the
effective rank of the update has no measurable effect on GSM8K or, within
our intervals, on its own benchmark. Rank measures of weight updates need a
noise baseline, and claims about alignment need a control without alignment
content.
\end{abstract}
