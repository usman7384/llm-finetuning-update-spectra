\begin{abstract}
Claims about what fine-tuning does to a language model's weights often rest on
the rank of the update $\dW = W_{\text{after}} - W_{\text{before}}$. We show that
three measures in common use, the number of singular values above
$0.01\,\sigma_1$, the rank needed for 90\% of the energy, and the entropy
effective rank, give nearly the same value for a real fine-tuning update as
for a random Gaussian matrix of the same shape. Stable rank,
$\|\dW\|_F^2/\|\dW\|_2^2$, separates the two by a factor of 1.6 to 9. We
establish this with a noise baseline for every weight matrix, on full SFT, DPO
and token-matched non-alignment fine-tunes of Qwen2.5-0.5B and SmolLM2-360M
(with seed repeats and layer-bootstrap intervals), and on seven publicly
released base/instruct pairs from 135M to 8B parameters. Fine-tuning updates
have a noise-like tail, which is why counting measures call them high rank,
and a few dominant directions, which is what stable rank detects. The
concentration is not specific to alignment: fine-tuning on Wikipedia text or
Python code is at least as concentrated as instruction tuning. As a case study
we revisit Timber, which cites an unchanged entropy effective rank between
base and instruct models as evidence that post-training is superficial. We
reproduce its measurement on two of its own model pairs, and find that a random
perturbation of the same norm leaves the effective rank just as unchanged.
Its eRank-based cutoff for editing $\dW$ touches about 1\% of the update's
energy, and a stable-rank cutoff that touches 59\% gives the same GSM8K
pass@$k$ within noise, while removing that 59\% outright costs 4 points of
pass@1.
\end{abstract}
