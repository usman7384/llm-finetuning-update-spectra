\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the measures are good for.} None of the five measures is
wrong; they answer different questions. Counting measures say how many
directions an update touches, and for fine-tuning the answer is ``almost
all''. Stable rank and the participation ratio say how unevenly the energy is
spread, and the answer is ``very''. The mistake is to read either number
without the value for noise next to it, or to take a property of all
fine-tuning as evidence about alignment.

\paragraph{Check where a checkpoint comes from.} One of the pairs we planned
to use, the OLMo 2 1B base and SFT checkpoints, turned out not to be a
fine-tuning pair at all: the two share no initialisation (\S\ref{sec:setup}).
Their difference still has a plausible-looking spectrum, with stable rank
4.4 times below noise. Nothing in a rank measure flags such a mistake; a
cosine between the two sets of weights does, and costs nothing.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Instruction SFT only.} Our controlled runs test instruction
  tuning on Alpaca-cleaned. Preference and reinforcement-learning training,
  part of what ``alignment'' usually means, appear only in the released OLMo 2
  stages, which are too small to measure above rounding.
  \item \textbf{Scale of our own training.} Our fine-tunes are 0.36B and 0.5B
  parameters with about two million tokens per epoch. The measurement results
  hold on released models up to 8B, but we do not study training at that scale.
  \item \textbf{Tokens, not steps.} The runs are matched on training tokens;
  the code runs take about 2.6 times fewer optimizer steps than the others.
  \item \textbf{Which null.} A Gaussian matrix is the least structured baseline
  possible. We add the pretrained weights as a second reference, and a
  shuffled-words fine-tune as a control for the data, but other baselines
  (for instance updates from other optimizers) could tell more.
  \item \textbf{Seeds.} Only the Qwen runs were repeated; the small task
  differences on SmolLM2 have no seed error bars.
  \item \textbf{Released models are black boxes,} apart from OLMo 2. Their
  data and recipes differ in many ways at once, so they test the measurement,
  not any claim about alignment.
  \item \textbf{Loss masking differs.} SFT computes the loss on answer tokens
  only, the controls on every token; the tokens seen are matched, the tokens
  trained on are not.
  \item \textbf{The shuffled-words result is unexplained.} The one explanation
  we tested, alignment with token frequency, is shared by other runs.
  \item \textbf{Shape, not direction.} We compare the spectra of updates, not
  the subspaces they occupy; two updates with the same stable rank can point
  in different directions.
  \item \textbf{The edit experiment is narrow:} one model, one benchmark,
  non-thinking mode, and $\lambda=0.5$ rather than Timber's recommended 0.2.
  \item \textbf{Perplexity is a crude measure of change.} It shows that the
  model's predictions move, not whether they get better or worse.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Rank measures that count directions describe fine-tuning updates as nearly
full rank and put them close to random matrices of the same shape. Measures
that weigh the top directions show the same updates to be far more
concentrated than noise, and more concentrated than the pretrained weights.
Both are right: the updates have a few strong directions and a long tail that
holds most of the energy. With training tokens held fixed, instruction
tuning, Wikipedia and code give updates of similar shape, and scrambled text
the most concentrated, so the shape is a property of fine-tuning rather than a
mark of instruction tuning. An unchanged effective rank of the weights after
post-training is what a random update of the same size also produces. Our
recommendation is simple: report any rank measure of a weight update next to
its value on a random matrix of the same shape, check that low-precision
training or storage did not erase the update, check that the two checkpoints
are really related, and test claims about alignment against a fine-tune
without alignment content.

\subsubsection*{Broader Impact Statement}
This paper is about measurement methodology for analysing fine-tuned language
models; it introduces no new capabilities or training methods. Its main
practical consequence is caution about reading weight-space statistics as
evidence that alignment is shallow or deep, since such claims can affect how
much trust is placed in alignment training.
