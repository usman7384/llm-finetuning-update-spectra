\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why are updates concentrated?} We did not set out to explain this,
but one likely reason is that the gradients of linear layers tend to be
approximately low rank during training \citep{zhao2024galore}, so a sum of
gradient steps inherits a few dominant directions. That would make
concentration a property of gradient-based fine-tuning in general, which fits
our finding that the data barely matters.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Which null.} A Gaussian matrix is the least structured
  baseline possible, and trained matrices are rarely that unstructured. We
  report the pretrained weights as a second reference for this reason; the
  updates are at least as concentrated as those.
  \item \textbf{Thresholds are arbitrary.} The threshold count's verdict
  depends on the cut-off: at $0.1\,\sigma_1$ instead of $0.01\,\sigma_1$ the
  Qwen SFT update scores 0.81 against 0.96 for noise
  (Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}). Having a free parameter that decides the
  conclusion is part of the problem.
  \item \textbf{Scale of our own training.} Our fine-tunes are 0.36B and 0.5B
  parameters with about two million tokens each. The measurement results hold
  on released models up to 8B, but we do not study training at that scale.
  \item \textbf{Released models are black boxes.} Their post-training data and
  recipes are unknown, so they test the measurement, not any claim about
  alignment.
  \item \textbf{Loss masking differs.} SFT computes the loss on answer tokens
  only, the controls on every token. The number of tokens seen is matched, the
  number trained on is not.
  \item \textbf{The cutoff test is narrow:} one model, one benchmark, one
  sampling mode.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Rank measures that count directions describe fine-tuning updates as nearly
full rank and put them close to random matrices of the same shape. Stable rank
shows the same updates to be several times more concentrated than noise. Both
are right: the updates have a few strong directions and a long tail that holds
most of the energy, and the tail matters to the model. The concentration is a
property of fine-tuning, not of alignment, and an unchanged effective rank
after post-training is what any small update produces. Our recommendation is
simple: report any rank measure of a weight update next to its value on a
random matrix of the same shape, and test claims about alignment against a
fine-tune without alignment content.

\subsubsection*{Broader Impact Statement}
This paper is about measurement methodology for analysing fine-tuned language
models; it introduces no new capabilities or training methods. Its main
practical consequence is caution about reading weight-space statistics as
evidence that alignment is shallow or deep, since such claims can affect how
much trust is placed in alignment training.
