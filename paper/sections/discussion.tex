\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the measures are good for.} None of the five measures is
wrong; they answer different questions. Counting measures say how many
directions an update touches, and for fine-tuning the answer is ``almost
all''. Stable rank and the participation ratio say how unevenly the energy is
spread, and the answer is ``very''. We do not recommend stable rank over the
others: it depends on a single direction, it is also far below noise for the
pretrained weights and for the difference of two unrelated checkpoints, not
only for a real update, and a cutoff built on it edited a model no better than
one built on eRank. The mistake is to read any one of these numbers without the value for
noise next to it, or to take a property of all fine-tuning as evidence about
alignment.

\paragraph{Check where a checkpoint comes from.} The obvious base for the
OLMo 2 1B SFT checkpoint, the main revision of the released base model, is not
its parent: the two share no initialisation, and the real parent is the final
mid-training checkpoint, published only as a branch (\S\ref{sec:setup}). The
difference between the unrelated pair still has a plausible-looking spectrum,
with stable rank 4.4 times below noise, much like the real update. Nothing in
a rank measure flags such a mistake; a cosine between the two sets of weights
does, and costs nothing.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One model for preference training.} The DPO runs and their
  swapped control are on Qwen2.5-0.5B only. Reinforcement learning appears only
  in the released OLMo 2 stage, which is too small to measure above rounding.
  \item \textbf{Scale of our own training.} Our fine-tunes are 0.36B and 0.5B
  parameters with about two million tokens per epoch. The measurement results
  hold on released models up to 8B, but we do not study training at that scale.
  \item \textbf{Tokens or steps.} Matching the training tokens leaves the
  code runs with 2.6--2.7 times fewer optimizer steps than SFT; matching the steps
  gives them 2.5--2.7 times more tokens. No single control matches both, and the
  two give different code updates.
  \item \textbf{Which null.} A Gaussian matrix is the least structured baseline
  possible, and even the difference of two unrelated trained checkpoints beats
  it. We add the pretrained weights as a harder reference, and matched
  fine-tunes without alignment content as the real test, but other baselines
  (for instance updates from other optimizers) could tell more.
  \item \textbf{Seeds.} The SFT, wikitext, code and DPO runs were repeated
  once; the shuffled-words and step-matched runs were not. Two seeds bound
  the run-to-run variation only roughly.
  \item \textbf{Released models are black boxes.} Apart from OLMo 2, their
  data and recipes differ in many ways at once, so they test the measurement,
  not any claim about alignment.
  \item \textbf{Loss masking differs.} SFT computes the loss on answer tokens
  only, the controls on every token; the tokens seen are matched, the tokens
  trained on are not.
  \item \textbf{The shuffled-words result is unexplained.} The one explanation
  we tested, alignment with token frequency, is shared by other runs.
  \item \textbf{Directions, measured coarsely.} The overlap of top-16
  subspaces shows that the data steers the directions of an update; it does
  not say which directions, or what they do.
  \item \textbf{The edit experiment is narrow:} one model, non-thinking mode
  only, and 198 GPQA questions, which leaves intervals of about $\pm3$
  points.
  \item \textbf{One random-update test.} The damage test uses one model,
  Qwen3-0.6B, and one task, GSM8K.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Rank measures that count directions describe fine-tuning updates as nearly
full rank and put them only a little below random matrices of the same shape.
Measures
that weigh the top directions show the same updates to be far more
concentrated than noise, and more concentrated than the pretrained weights.
Both are right: the updates have a few strong directions and a long tail that
holds most of the energy. With the amount of training held fixed, the data
shapes an update somewhat, but instruction tuning does not stand out from
Wikipedia or code; DPO gives the same shape and directions with real or
half-swapped preferences; and scrambled text gives the most concentrated
update. The shape is a property of fine-tuning, not a mark of alignment. An
unchanged effective rank of the weights after post-training is
what a random update of the same size also produces, and an edit built on
the effective rank of the update does nothing measurable on two benchmarks.
Our
recommendation is simple: report any rank measure of a weight update next to
its value on a random matrix of the same shape, check that low-precision
training or storage did not erase the update, check that the two checkpoints
are really related, and test claims about alignment against a fine-tune
without alignment content.

\subsubsection*{Broader Impact Statement}
This paper is about measurement methodology for analysing fine-tuned language
models; it introduces no new capabilities or training methods. Its main
practical consequence is caution about reading weight-space statistics as
evidence that alignment is shallow or deep, since such claims can affect how
much trust is placed in alignment training.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility}
Every run records its configuration, its exact training rows, its seed, its
token count and the fraction of weights left unchanged. The code, the
configurations and run manifests, the saved singular-value spectra of every
update, the random-update seeds, the edit scripts and the evaluation prompts
are at \url{https://github.com/usman7384/llm-finetuning-update-spectra}.
